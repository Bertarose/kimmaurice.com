Topless at the spa: a rule for whom?

Can queer, trans and non-binary people, or anyone whose body doesn’t fit the boxes, enjoy a spa without covering their chest? Here is what the spas answered in writing, and what the others post on their websites.

Having to wear a swimsuit is one thing. Having to cover your chest is another.

A bit of history

Less than a hundred years ago, men had to cover their chests too. The rule changed for them. Not for women.

  1. Ancient RomeCold bath, warm room, hot bath: already our spa circuit. Depending on the period, women and men bathed separately or together. Emperor Hadrian banned mixed bathing; it didn’t last.
  2. Paris, Middle AgesSeveral dozen étuves, public steam baths, much like a hammam.
  3. FinlandPeople generally go to the sauna naked, women and men separately in public saunas. For centuries.
  4. New Jersey, 1891A man is fined for an “indecent” bathing suit. In 1910, tops are still mandatory for men.
  5. 1930sMen fight to go topless at the beach. By 1939, 67% of the public approves.
  6. Saint-Sauveur, 1960sQuebec’s first Nordic spa begins in the sauna of a private house.
  7. TodayMore than 100 Nordic spas in Quebec. The swimsuit-top rule is written on none of the 18 websites checked.

Sources: History.com; Livres Hebdo (Georges Vigarello, Le propre et le sale); Visit Finland; Historical Society of Ocean Grove; Strøm spa nordique; Tourisme Montréal. Full links in the PDF report.

The method

From November 1 to 16, 2026, 19 establishments were contacted by email. Eighteen spas with shared facilities are part of the investigation; Spa Ovarium, which offers only individual treatments, was set aside. All received the same question: can you use the facilities topless, whether you are a man, a woman, or a person whose body doesn’t fit these categories? Quotes are reproduced word for word (translated from French here). Each spa quoted received its text with the chance to correct it before publication. La Source asked that its emails not be quoted: its profile summarizes the rule without quoting them.

The websites of 18 spas were also checked. None says whether the chest must be covered.

On November 19, I also looked for what each spa says about access for people with reduced mobility, on its website and in the assessments of Kéroul, an organization that visits places to measure their accessibility (its rating is the only one recognized by Quebec’s Ministry of Tourism). No website says its baths are wheelchair accessible, and eleven do not mention it at all. Call before your visit.

To plan an accessible outing, Kéroul publishes the details of each place it has assessed: keroul.qc.ca.

I did not press the spas that chose not to answer. For them, the profile summarizes what their website says. The data comes from two sources only: what the spas wrote to me, and what they post on their websites. The goal is to inform, not to judge the answers.

What remains to be assessed

This directory settles no legal question. Since 2016, Quebec’s Charter of human rights and freedoms has prohibited discrimination based on sex and on gender identity or expression. Can a spa require a top for women and not for men? The question deserves to be put to lawyers and to the CDPDJ, Quebec’s human rights commission.

About Sans étiquette

Sans étiquette (“no label”) is a series of investigations and resources for queer, trans and non-binary people, and for everyone whose body or identity doesn’t fit the boxes. Topless at the spa is its first investigation.

The investigation, the emails and the editorial choices are by Kim Maurice. Artificial intelligence tools were used to sort the replies, lay out the pages and proofread. Every quote was checked against the original emails.
